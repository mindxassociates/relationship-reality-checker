How to use this tool

Look for patterns—not a perfect relationship

This checker helps you recognize potentially toxic, manipulative, or controlling relationship patterns and consider safer ways to respond. It is not a test you need to “pass,” and one difficult interaction does not define an entire relationship.

1Think of one relationshipChoose a current or past relationship you want to understand.
2Notice repetition and impactFocus on what keeps happening, how it affects you, and what follows when you speak up.
3Choose one useful next stepTry a boundary, document facts safely, seek support, or make a safety plan.
Common relationship conflict

Conflict can still allow connection

  • Both people can disagree and be heard.
  • Each person can take responsibility.
  • Boundaries may disappoint someone but are respected.
  • Repair leads to meaningful, sustained change.

Helpful response: Slow down, communicate clearly, listen, negotiate, and repair.

Possible toxic pattern

Control replaces mutual problem-solving

  • You are repeatedly blamed, confused, afraid, or diminished.
  • Your reality is denied or rewritten.
  • Boundaries trigger punishment, threats, ridicule, or retaliation.
  • Apologies repeatedly fail to produce change.

Helpful response: Prioritize clarity, outside support, boundaries, and safety over winning the argument.

Keep context in mind: Stress, misunderstandings, and poor communication happen in ordinary relationships. The key questions are whether the behavior forms a pattern, whether power is used to limit your choices, and whether both people can participate safely in repair.

First, a steadying frame

Confusion can be information

Repeated manipulation can make you second-guess memory, judgment, and feelings. Pause before explaining it away. Look for patterns, impact, accountability, and whether it feels safe to disagree.

The cycle may repeat—and may change

1. Tension

Stress, monitoring, walking on eggshells, attempts to prevent an outburst.

2. Incident

Verbal, emotional, physical, sexual, digital, or financial harm.

3. Reconnection

Apologies, affection, promises, gifts, or pressure to forgive.

4. Calm

A quieter period that can restore hope before tension returns.

Not every abusive relationship follows four neat phases. The calm phase may shorten or disappear. Responsibility always belongs to the person choosing harmful behavior.

FOG detector

Fear, obligation, and guilt

Fear — “Something bad will happen”

Threats, intimidation, exposure, abandonment, retaliation, or “no one will believe you.” Ask: Am I choosing, or trying to prevent a consequence?

Obligation — “I owe them”

Money, gifts, time, sacrifice, or relationship roles are used as debts. Ask: Would a freely given choice require repayment through compliance?

Guilt — “I am bad if I say no”

Your limit is reframed as cruelty, selfishness, or betrayal. Ask: Can I care about their feelings without abandoning my boundary?

A quick reality test

Healthy influence allows questions, time, disagreement, and a genuine no. Coercion narrows your choices through fear or penalty.

Boundary reset

From escalation to a safer response

1

Notice and pause

Feel your feet. Take three slower breaths. “I’m going to pause so I can think clearly.”

2

State the limit

“I’m willing to talk when there is no yelling.” A boundary names what you will do.

3

Watch the response

Respect, curiosity, and repair are different from blame, ridicule, punishment, or escalation.

4

Choose the next safe step

Continue, take space, contact support, document safely, or leave. Co-regulation is only appropriate when both people can act safely.

Safety first

If you feel unsafe

Trust urgency. If there is immediate danger, contact local emergency services when safe to do so. Consider a trusted person, a safer room or exit, essential documents/medications, a code word, and a device the other person cannot access.
